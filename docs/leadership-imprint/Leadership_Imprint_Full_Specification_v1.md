# PART ONE · THE FRAMEWORK

---

## 1.1 What this instrument is

The Leadership Imprint measures **how a person leads** across two behavioural axes, then measures
**what happens to that when the pressure comes on**, and finally reads **what a team of these people
is missing**.

It is a behavioural instrument, not a personality test. It describes what somebody does, not who
they are underneath.

### The core belief

> **A fleet needs all four. Nobody argues that a tractor should be a rocket.**

Too many of one style does not produce a strong team. It produces a *predictable failure* — and
each of the four imbalances fails in its own specific, nameable way.

### Five layers

| Layer | What it reports | Who else does this |
|---|---|---|
| **Your Imprint** | Where you sit on pace and priority | Everyone |
| **Under Pressure** | Where you move when it is hard | Hogan, at enterprise price |
| **Your Range** | The distance between those two points | Nobody, cleanly |
| **Working with the other three** | How to lead people unlike you | Rarely, and never well |
| **The Fleet** | What your team is missing and what it costs | **Nobody** |

**Layers three and five are the product.** Everything else is table stakes.

---

## 1.2 The two axes

### PACE — how quickly you move from decision to action

**MEASURED** ←──────────────────────→ **FAST**

Not speed of thought. Speed of *commitment*. A measured leader may think very fast and still want
another day before they act.

| Measured end | Fast end |
|---|---|
| Wants the whole picture first | Decides on the picture available |
| Comfortable saying "not yet" | Uncomfortable with unresolved things |
| Prefers to be right | Prefers to be moving |
| Late commitment, high follow-through | Early commitment, adjusts on the way |

⚠️ **Neither end is better and the report must never imply otherwise.** Measured is not slow.
Fast is not reckless.

### PRIORITY — what you protect first when two things compete

**TASK** ←──────────────────────→ **PEOPLE**

Not whether you care about people. Everyone does. This is which one you protect when they cannot
both be protected.

| Task end | People end |
|---|---|
| Protects the outcome | Protects the person |
| "What has to be true by Friday" | "Who is carrying this" |
| Reads relationship as a means | Reads outcome as a means |
| Trusts the plan | Trusts the room |

⚠️ **Task-priority is not cold and people-priority is not soft.** The report must say this out loud,
because every reader will assume otherwise about whichever end they did not land on.

---

## 1.3 The four styles

```
                     TASK PRIORITY              PEOPLE PRIORITY

    FAST PACE          ROCKET                       JET

    MEASURED PACE      TRACTOR                      BUS
```

**Naming rationale.** Vehicles rather than letters or colours, for three reasons. They carry
observable behaviour rather than virtue, so nobody is flattered into a box. One of them is
deliberately unglamorous, which makes the set honest. And they are inherently about *movement* —
getting from A to B and taking people with you, which is what leadership is.

---

## 1.4 The third dimension — RANGE

**How far you move from your home position when the situation demands it.**

Range is measured as **the distance between your natural position and your pressure position**, not
as a separate block of questions.

This is borrowed structurally from the natural-versus-adapted graph in TTI-style instruments, but
pointed at *pressure* rather than *environment* — which is more useful for leadership, and is the
thing no comparable instrument reports.

### Why it must not be a virtue

⚠️ **Range is not "flex" and high range is not better.** Both ends have a cost:

| High range | Low range |
|---|---|
| Adapts to what the situation needs | Utterly predictable |
| **Cost:** reads as inconsistent. People cannot anticipate you. Under stress you may become someone your team does not recognise | **Cost:** the same response regardless of what is in front of you. When the situation calls for something else, you do not have it |

If range were presented as a strength, one quadrant would quietly become superior, and every
reader in the other three would notice.

### Direction matters more than distance

**Which way you move under pressure is more diagnostic than how far.** A Bus who moves toward
Rocket under pressure is a completely different leader from a Bus who moves further into Bus.

The report names both the distance and the direction.

---

## 1.5 What this instrument deliberately does not do

- **It does not measure competence.** A Rocket and a Tractor can both be excellent or both be poor.
- **It does not measure emotional intelligence, values, motivation or mindset.** Those are different
  instruments measuring different things.
- **It does not predict performance.** It describes behaviour.
- **It is not a hiring filter.** Self-report instruments are unsuitable for selection decisions and
  the report says so explicitly.
- **It does not have a right answer.** No style, no position, no range is presented as better.

⚠️ **This section appears in the report, near the front.** Candour about limits is what separates a
credible instrument from a horoscope, and it is the thing Belbin does well.

# PART TWO · DESIGN NOTES

---

## 2.1 Question design rules

These are binding. A question that breaks one of these is a broken question.

**1 · Describe behaviour, never virtue.**
*"I make decisions quickly"* is answerable. *"I am a decisive leader"* is a compliment somebody
will agree with.

**2 · No option may be obviously correct.**
Test: would a competent, respected leader choose each option for good reasons? If not, the item is
dead and the data it produces is noise.

**3 · Both options in a forced choice must be genuinely attractive.**
If one sounds like the good leader, everybody picks it.

**4 · Present tense, real situations.**
*"When a deadline moves up, I…"* not *"I believe that…"*

**5 · No industry language.**
This has to work identically for a hospital, a warehouse, a school and a bank. No shifts, no
patients, no customers, no stations. **Use: team, deadline, project, meeting, plan, workload.**

**6 · Specific enough to picture, general enough to fit.**
⚠️ *"When a shift is short-staffed"* is too narrow. *"When resources are tight"* is too vague.
**"When the team is short-handed and the work still has to get done"** is right.

**7 · Grade 7–8 reading level.**
Plain and human. Contractions are fine. **No em dashes.** Short sentences.

**8 · One idea per question.**
*"I decide quickly and communicate clearly"* measures two things and scores neither.

---

## 2.2 Question formats

**FORCED CHOICE (A/B)** — used for natural position.
Two statements. Pick the one more true of you. **No neutral option**, which prevents the midpoint
clustering that ruins most self-report instruments.

**FREQUENCY SCALE (1–5)** — used for pressure and validity items.
*Almost never · Rarely · Sometimes · Often · Almost always*

**SCENARIO (A/B/C/D)** — used for pressure position.
A situation, four responses, one per style. Harder to game than direct questions, and it makes the
pressure block feel genuinely different from the natural block rather than repetitive.

---

## 2.3 Block structure — 40 questions

| Block | Items | Format | Measures |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | 10 | Forced choice | Natural PACE |
| **B** | 10 | Forced choice | Natural PRIORITY |
| **C** | 12 | Scenario | Pressure position (both axes) |
| **D** | 5 | Frequency | Validity and consistency |
| **E** | 3 | Forced choice | ⚠️ Conflict default — **scored separately, withheld** |
| | **40** | | |

**Estimated completion: 12 to 15 minutes.**

### Block E is held back

Block E measures conflict default. It is **not included in the standard individual report.**

It is released either at a facilitated session (Day 2 of a workshop, where difficult conversations
are the subject) or as a separate unlock for individual users.

⚠️ **This is a deliberate product decision.** It gives a facilitated session something the
self-serve report does not have, which is what makes the facilitated version worth paying for.

---

## 2.4 Anti-gaming design

⚠️ **Randomised option order. Required, not optional.** In every forced-choice item, randomise
which side each option appears on. If option A is always the "fast" answer, somebody clicking A
forty times produces a clean Rocket result rather than a flag. **Store which option was presented
in which position, per item.** Without this, straight-lining is undetectable in the forced-choice
blocks.

**Reverse-scored items.** At least 3 items in Block A and 3 in Block B are reverse-scored, so a
respondent cannot pattern-answer down one side.

**Scenario format for pressure.** In a scenario, the "good" answer is not obvious because all four
responses are things real leaders do.

**Validity items (Block D).** Five frequency items that check internal consistency. If somebody's
Block D answers contradict their Block A and B pattern, the report flags **low confidence** rather
than reporting a style with false precision.

**Straight-line detection.** If a respondent selects the same position on every frequency item, or
picks option A on every forced choice, the report returns a low-confidence result and invites a
retake.

---

## 2.5 Reading level and voice

**Grade 7 to 8. Plain, human, direct.**

- Contractions are fine and preferred
- **No em dashes anywhere in learner-facing text**
- Short sentences
- Second person throughout: *you*, not *the leader* or *the respondent*
- Never clinical. This is written by a person who has led people, not by a psychometrician

**What this must not sound like:** *"Individuals scoring in the upper quartile of the Pace dimension
demonstrate a propensity toward accelerated decision commitment."*

**What it should sound like:** *"You move fast. When something needs deciding, you would rather
decide and adjust than wait and be sure."*

# PART THREE · THE 40 QUESTIONS

---

## BLOCK A · NATURAL PACE
### 10 items · forced choice · measures Measured ←→ Fast

**Scoring:** each item scores 1 point to either MEASURED or FAST. Reverse-scored items marked ⟲.

---

**A1.** Which is more true of you?
- **(a)** I would rather decide now with most of the information → **FAST**
- **(b)** I would rather wait until I have all of it → **MEASURED**

**A2.** When a plan changes at short notice:
- **(a)** I start moving and work out the details as I go → **FAST**
- **(b)** I want to understand what changed before I move → **MEASURED**

**A3.** ⟲ Which is more true?
- **(a)** I am often the last one in the room to commit to a direction → **MEASURED**
- **(b)** I am often the first one in the room to commit to a direction → **FAST**

**A4.** In a meeting where a decision needs making:
- **(a)** I am usually pushing for us to land it today → **FAST**
- **(b)** I am usually the one asking what we have not considered → **MEASURED**

**A5.** When somebody brings me a problem:
- **(a)** My first instinct is to ask questions → **MEASURED**
- **(b)** My first instinct is to suggest a next step → **FAST**

**A6.** ⟲ I would rather:
- **(a)** Have to redo something because I moved too early → **FAST**
- **(b)** Have to explain why we have not started yet → **MEASURED**

**A7.** Given a new project with an unclear brief:
- **(a)** I would start something and let it clarify as we go → **FAST**
- **(b)** I would get the brief clarified before we start → **MEASURED**

**A8.** People who work with me would more likely say:
- **(a)** Things move quickly around me → **FAST**
- **(b)** Things are thought through around me → **MEASURED**

**A9.** ⟲ When I look back at decisions I regret, they are more often:
- **(a)** Ones I made too quickly → **FAST**
- **(b)** Ones I sat on too long → **MEASURED**

**A10.** My inbox and my task list:
- **(a)** I work through them in order and finish what I start → **MEASURED**
- **(b)** I move to whatever matters most right now → **FAST**

---

## BLOCK B · NATURAL PRIORITY
### 10 items · forced choice · measures Task ←→ People

---

**B1.** When the two conflict, I protect:
- **(a)** The deadline → **TASK**
- **(b)** The person → **PEOPLE**

**B2.** Someone on my team is underperforming during a busy period. My first move:
- **(a)** Find out what is going on with them → **PEOPLE**
- **(b)** Work out how to cover the gap → **TASK**

**B3.** ⟲ I would rather be described as:
- **(a)** Someone you can count on to deliver → **TASK**
- **(b)** Someone you can count on to understand → **PEOPLE**

**B4.** In a meeting that is running over:
- **(a)** I am watching the clock → **TASK**
- **(b)** I am watching who has not spoken → **PEOPLE**

**B5.** When I take on a new team, the first thing I want to know is:
- **(a)** What is not working → **TASK**
- **(b)** Who is who → **PEOPLE**

**B6.** ⟲ A project finished on time but the team is exhausted. My honest first reaction:
- **(a)** Relief that we made it → **TASK**
- **(b)** Concern about what that cost them → **PEOPLE**

**B7.** My office or workspace:
- **(a)** Is set up for me to work → **TASK**
- **(b)** Is set up for people to come in → **PEOPLE**

**B8.** When I give feedback, I am more focused on:
- **(a)** Being clear about what needs to change → **TASK**
- **(b)** Making sure it lands without damage → **PEOPLE**

**B9.** ⟲ I find it harder to:
- **(a)** Have a difficult conversation with someone I like → **PEOPLE**
- **(b)** Slow down for a conversation when the work is behind → **TASK**

**B10.** If I could only get one right this week:
- **(a)** Every commitment met → **TASK**
- **(b)** Every person on my team feeling supported → **PEOPLE**

---

## BLOCK C · UNDER PRESSURE
### 12 items · scenario, four options · measures pressure position on both axes

**Scoring:** each option maps to one style. **ROCKET** = Fast+Task, **JET** = Fast+People,
**TRACTOR** = Measured+Task, **BUS** = Measured+People.

⚠️ **Scenarios are deliberately industry-neutral.** No shifts, patients, customers or stations.
Every scenario has to make equal sense to a warehouse supervisor and a hospital manager.

---

**C1.** Two of your team are off unexpectedly and the work still has to get done today. You:
- **(a)** Reassign it yourself within the hour and tell people what changed → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Pull the team together fast, work out who can take what → **JET**
- **(c)** Work out exactly what will and will not get done, then communicate it → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Check in with the people who are left before you ask more of them → **BUS**

**C2.** Something has gone wrong and you do not yet know why. You:
- **(a)** Contain it first, understand it later → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Get the people closest to it talking, quickly → **JET**
- **(c)** Stop and find out what actually happened before doing anything → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Make sure nobody is being blamed while you work it out → **BUS**

**C3.** Your manager changes a priority with no explanation. You:
- **(a)** Change direction and move → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Reframe it for the team so it makes sense to them → **JET**
- **(c)** Ask for the reasoning before you pass it on → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Absorb the frustration yourself rather than pass it down → **BUS**

**C4.** A deadline has moved up and it is now very tight. You:
- **(a)** Cut scope, decide what goes, announce it → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Rally the team, find a way round → **JET**
- **(c)** Map exactly what is achievable and commit only to that → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Ask the team what is realistic before committing to anything → **BUS**

**C5.** Someone on your team makes a significant mistake. Your first move:
- **(a)** Fix the consequence, address the person after → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Talk to them straight away, keep it moving → **JET**
- **(c)** Understand how it happened before responding to it → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Check how they are before anything else → **BUS**

**C6.** You are in a meeting where the mood has turned. You:
- **(a)** Name the problem and push for a decision → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Change your approach in the moment to shift the room → **JET**
- **(c)** Let it play out, then follow up individually afterwards → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Slow it down, make space for people to say what is wrong → **BUS**

**C7.** You have been given a target you do not think is achievable. You:
- **(a)** Take it on and work out how afterwards → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Negotiate it, but keep everyone positive while you do → **JET**
- **(c)** Build the case for why it is not achievable, with evidence → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Worry first about what it will do to the team → **BUS**

**C8.** Two people on your team are in open conflict. You:
- **(a)** Make a decision about the issue and move everyone on → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Get them talking quickly, before it hardens → **JET**
- **(c)** Speak to each of them separately and get the full picture → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Give it a little time and stay close to both of them → **BUS**

**C9.** Under real pressure, the thing you are most likely to stop doing is:
- **(a)** Consulting people before deciding → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Following the plan you agreed → **JET**
- **(c)** Communicating what you are thinking → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Saying the difficult thing that needs saying → **BUS**

**C10.** When you are stretched too thin, you are most likely to:
- **(a)** Push harder and expect the same from everyone → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Take on more than you can deliver → **JET**
- **(c)** Go quiet and work through it alone → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Carry other people's load as well as your own → **BUS**

**C11.** A decision has to be made now and the information is incomplete. You:
- **(a)** Decide → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** Decide, and get people behind it fast → **JET**
- **(c)** Decide the smallest thing you can, and hold the rest → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** Check who this affects before deciding → **BUS**

**C12.** Looking back at the last time things got genuinely difficult, the feedback you would most
likely have got is:
- **(a)** You moved too fast for people to keep up → **ROCKET**
- **(b)** You promised more than could be delivered → **JET**
- **(c)** Nobody knew what you were thinking → **TRACTOR**
- **(d)** You did not say the thing that needed saying → **BUS**

## BLOCK D · VALIDITY AND CONSISTENCY
### 5 items · frequency scale 1–5

**These do not contribute to style scoring.** They check whether the result can be trusted.

**Scale:** 1 Almost never · 2 Rarely · 3 Sometimes · 4 Often · 5 Almost always

---

**D1.** I change how I work depending on who I am working with.
*(Range corroboration — should correlate with measured range)*

**D2.** People who work with me know what to expect from me.
*(Reverse range corroboration — should inversely correlate)*

**D3.** I behave differently when things are going badly than when they are going well.
*(Pressure-shift corroboration — should correlate with natural-to-pressure distance)*

**D4.** I answered these questions about how I actually behave, not how I would like to.
*(Self-report honesty check)*

**D5.** I found some of these questions hard to answer because it depends on the situation.
*(High score plus low measured range flags a possible response-set issue)*

## BLOCK E · CONFLICT DEFAULT
### 3 items · forced choice · ⚠️ SCORED SEPARATELY AND WITHHELD

Maps loosely to conflict-mode behaviour. **Not reported in the standard individual report.**

---

**E1.** When a disagreement gets uncomfortable, I am more likely to:
- **(a)** Push for my position → **ASSERT**
- **(b)** Look for something we can both live with → **ACCOMMODATE**

**E2.** When somebody is underperforming and I have noticed but not said anything, it is usually
because:
- **(a)** I am waiting for the right moment → **DELAY**
- **(b)** I am hoping it resolves itself → **AVOID**

**E3.** After a difficult conversation, I am more likely to think:
- **(a)** I should have been clearer → **UNDER-ASSERT**
- **(b)** I should have been softer → **OVER-ASSERT**

# PART FOUR · SCORING

---

## 4.1 Natural position

**PACE score** = (FAST responses in Block A) ÷ 10 × 100
**PRIORITY score** = (PEOPLE responses in Block B) ÷ 10 × 100

Both produce **0 to 100**.

- PACE: 0 = fully measured, 100 = fully fast
- PRIORITY: 0 = fully task, 100 = fully people

**Natural style** is the quadrant the point falls into:

| PACE | PRIORITY | Style |
|---|---|---|
| ≥ 50 | < 50 | **ROCKET** |
| ≥ 50 | ≥ 50 | **JET** |
| < 50 | < 50 | **TRACTOR** |
| < 50 | ≥ 50 | **BUS** |

---

## 4.2 Blend percentages

⚠️ **Do not report a bare quadrant.** Report a primary style with supporting influences, the way
the Brand Playbook does. A bare four-box output feels cruder than a product you already ship.

**Calculate distance from the point to each quadrant centre.** Quadrant centres are at (25,25),
(25,75), (75,25), (75,75).

```
for each style S:
    d(S) = euclidean distance from (PACE, PRIORITY) to centre(S)
    affinity(S) = 1 / (d(S) + 10)      # +10 prevents divide-by-zero spikes
    
blend(S) = affinity(S) / sum(all affinities) × 100
```

**Report:** primary style percentage, plus any supporting style at 10% or above.

*Example output:* **68% Tractor**, with **19% Bus** and **13% Rocket**.

---

## 4.3 Pressure position

Block C produces 12 style selections. Convert to a coordinate:

```
FAST_count    = count(ROCKET) + count(JET)
PEOPLE_count  = count(JET) + count(BUS)

PRESSURE_PACE     = FAST_count / 12 × 100
PRESSURE_PRIORITY = PEOPLE_count / 12 × 100
```

**Pressure style** = the quadrant that point falls into, same thresholds as 4.1.

---

## 4.4 Range

```
RANGE = euclidean distance between (PACE, PRIORITY) and (PRESSURE_PACE, PRESSURE_PRIORITY)
```

Maximum possible distance is ~141. Bands:

| Distance | Band | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0 – 15 | **Anchored** | You are the same person under pressure. Highly predictable |
| 16 – 30 | **Steady** | Small shift. Recognisably you, slightly adjusted |
| 31 – 50 | **Adaptive** | Real movement. You become noticeably different |
| 51 + | **Wide** | Large shift. Your team may not recognise you under pressure |

⚠️ **No band is better.** Each description in the report names the cost as well as the value.

**Direction** is reported separately and is more diagnostic than distance:
*"Under pressure you move toward Rocket"* is the finding, not the number.

---

## 4.5 Confidence flags

The report returns **LOW CONFIDENCE** and invites a retake if any of these are true:

| Condition | Why |
|---|---|
| All Block A answers on the same side AND all Block B answers on the same side | Almost certainly a response set, not a profile |
| Block D straight-lined (identical value on all five) | Not engaging with the items |
| D4 ≤ 2 | Self-reported that they did not answer honestly |
| Block C dominated by one option letter (≥ 10 of 12) | Pattern-answering |
| PACE and PRIORITY both between 45 and 55 | Genuinely centred. **Not an error** — report as "balanced" with a note that the styles will feel equally true, and recommend a facilitated debrief |

---

## 4.6 The Fleet

**Two input paths, identical logic.**

### Path A — self-estimated

After the main assessment, four short questions:

> Think about the people who report to you. Roughly how many of them would you say are:
> - Fast-moving and task-first? *(Rocket)*
> - Fast-moving and people-first? *(Jet)*
> - Measured and task-first? *(Tractor)*
> - Measured and people-first? *(Bus)*

Report flags this as an estimate.

### Path B — calculated

Every team member takes the assessment. Manager receives a fleet report built from actual primary
styles. **This is the version an organisation pays for.**

### Fleet logic

```
total = sum of all four counts
share(S) = count(S) / total × 100

DOMINANT  : any style ≥ 50%
HEAVY     : any style 35–49%
MISSING   : any style = 0%
THIN      : any style > 0% but ≤ 15%
BALANCED  : no style ≥ 35% and no style = 0%
```

### What each finding triggers

**DOMINANT or HEAVY:**

| Style | Finding |
|---|---|
| **Tractor** | Nothing ships. Everything gets studied. The team is permanently nearly ready, and the plan is always one revision from finished |
| **Bus** | Lovely to be on, going nowhere. Meetings run long. Hard decisions get deferred to protect the atmosphere. Underperformance gets absorbed instead of addressed |
| **Jet** | Constant motion, no depth. Direction changes weekly. Everyone is flexing and nobody is holding a line. Commitments outnumber capacity |
| **Rocket** | Speed and casualties. Things get done and people leave. Nobody says the risky thing because there is no room to |

**MISSING or THIN:**

| Style | Cost |
|---|---|
| **No Tractor** | Nobody checks. The flaw gets found by a customer |
| **No Bus** | Nobody knows how people actually are. You find out at the exit interview |
| **No Jet** | Nothing adapts. The plan meets reality and the plan wins |
| **No Rocket** | Nothing ships on time. Nobody says the hard thing |

**Also report the predictable friction pairs** where both styles are present at 20% or more:

- **Rocket ↔ Tractor** — the sharpest. One thinks the other is reckless, one thinks the other is
  slow, and both are right about the risk they can see
- **Bus ↔ Rocket** — the Rocket reads the Bus as soft, the Bus reads the Rocket as careless with
  people. Most likely to become a formal grievance
- **Jet ↔ Tractor** — the Tractor sees improvising where the Jet sees adapting
- **Bus ↔ Jet** — the quietest, easiest to miss. Both people-first so it looks like alignment,
  until the Bus finds out the Jet promised something on their behalf

# PART FIVE · THE FOUR PROFILES

**Written at report depth. This is where the quality lives.**

---
---

## THE TRACTOR
### Measured pace · Task priority

### How you move

Slowly, deliberately, and in a straight line.

You want the whole story before you start. Not because you are cautious by temperament, but because
you have learned that starting without it costs more later. Getting from A to B takes you longer
than it takes most people around you.

And when it is done, it is done properly. Fly over the field afterwards and the rows are perfectly
straight.

### What people notice

You ask the question everyone else skipped. Usually about halfway through a meeting, usually the
one that changes the answer.

You want context before you want the ask. Somebody who comes to you with "can you just do this
quickly" gets a series of questions back, and they sometimes read that as resistance. It is not
resistance. You are trying to do it properly and you cannot until you understand it.

Your desk and your inbox may be chaos while your actual work is immaculate. That surprises people
who assume order shows up everywhere. Your order is in the output.

### Your gift

**Nothing falls through.**

When you say something is done, it is done. Checked, complete, and it will hold. Your team learns
they do not have to go behind you.

You find the flaw in the plan while it is still cheap to fix. Everyone else finds it in week six.
That is not pessimism, though it gets read that way. It is you running the whole thing through
before committing to it.

And there is a kind of trust that only you build. Not warmth. **Reliability** — the knowledge that
what you said would happen is what will happen.

### Under pressure

**You stop.**

Detail becomes somewhere to hide from a decision. The need for the whole story becomes a reason not
to move, and the more pressure there is, the more information you want before you commit.

Meanwhile the team is waiting. And here is the part that is hard to see from inside it: **they
often cannot tell the difference between you thinking and you being stuck.** From the outside both
look like silence.

Your other pressure move is going quiet. You work the problem alone rather than out loud, because
talking about it before you have an answer feels premature. Your team experiences that as being
shut out at exactly the moment they most needed to know what you were thinking.

### What you need from other people

**Context, and lead time.**

Ambush a Tractor with a decision and you will get one of two things. Either a freeze, or a yes that
quietly does not happen.

Give the same Tractor the background on Monday and the decision on Wednesday and you get a better
answer than anyone else in the building would have given you.

### What drives you mad

Being rushed. Being told to just make it work. Discovering that a decision was made without the
information you would have provided, and then being asked to fix the consequences.

Meetings that reach a conclusion nobody has thought through.

### The thing worth working on

**Say what you are thinking before you have finished thinking it.**

Not the answer. The process. *"I am not there yet, here is what I am weighing, I will have it by
Thursday."*

That one sentence costs you nothing and it changes everything for the people waiting on you.
Silence reads as absence. Narration reads as leadership.

---

## THE BUS
### Measured pace · People priority

### How you move

Steadily, and with everybody on board.

Point A to point B through the middle of the city, stopping for passengers, taking a detour when
somebody needs one, then getting back on route. You arrive later than the direct route would. You
arrive with everyone.

### What people notice

The bowl of something on your desk. Photos up. Your door open because you want it open, not
because policy says so.

People stop by without an agenda, and you let them. **You invite the distraction.** That is not a
failure of focus. That is the job as you understand it, and you are right more often than the
people who think otherwise.

Warmth in how you write. You soften things. You ask how somebody is before you ask what you called
about.

### Your gift

**Nobody leaves a Bus.**

That is the plainest way to say it. Retention on your team is better than it should be given
everything else, and it is because people feel known.

You know what is going on with people long before it becomes anybody's formal problem. The
resignation that surprises everyone else does not surprise you, because you saw it three weeks ago
and had a conversation about it.

You build the trust that everything else stands on. A team that trusts its leader will do hard
things. A team that does not will do only what it is told, exactly.

### Under pressure

**You absorb.**

You take the hit rather than pass it down. You protect your team from information they may actually
have needed. And you go quiet when something needs saying.

Here is the difficult truth about your pressure pattern: **it looks like kindness and it functions
like avoidance.**

The conversation you did not have because the timing was not right is a standard you have now set.
The underperformance you carried yourself is a message the rest of the team received clearly. And
the person you were protecting from a hard conversation is the person who did not get the chance to
fix it.

Nobody experiences you as a coward. Everybody experiences the consequence.

### What you need from other people

**Relationship first, task second.**

A Bus who does not know where they stand with you will read every neutral message as a problem.
Two minutes of human contact before the ask is not a courtesy with you. It is the thing that makes
the ask land.

### What drives you mad

Being treated as a resource. Decisions made without regard for what they will cost the people who
have to absorb them. Somebody being written off before anyone tried.

Speed for its own sake.

### The thing worth working on

**Say the thing.**

Not harder. Not colder. **Earlier.**

The version of the conversation you have at week two is kind. The version you have at week ten,
after it has become a problem, is not, however gently you phrase it.

Your instinct to protect people is right. Aim it at the person who needs to hear the truth while
there is still time to act on it.

## THE JET
### Fast pace · People priority

### How you move

Fast, and around things.

Same A to B as everybody else, but you will divert around weather, re-route mid-flight, and arrive
on time by a path nobody planned. The obstacle does not stop you and it barely slows you.

### What people notice

You read the room before you speak. You change register depending on who is in front of you, and it
is not performance. It is calibration. The message stays the same and the delivery changes.

You are usually the first to notice a meeting has gone wrong, often before anybody says so.

You move quickly and people come with you, which is a rarer combination than it sounds. Most fast
leaders leave people behind. You do not.

### Your gift

**Agility with people.**

You get a difficult message across because you worked out how that specific person needed to hear
it. Somebody else delivers the same message and it lands as an attack. You deliver it and it lands
as help.

You are often the bridge. Between a Rocket and everyone else. Between the plan and the reality.
Between what leadership said and what the team can actually hear.

And when things change suddenly, you are already moving. No grieving the old plan, no needing it
explained twice.

### Under pressure

**You over-promise.**

Speed plus wanting people to be alright produces commitments that cannot all be kept. You say yes
to two incompatible things and you believe both at the time you say them. That is the specific
trap: you are not being dishonest, you are being optimistic at speed.

Your other pressure move is abandoning the plan. You flex naturally, so under pressure you flex
more, and at some point adapting becomes improvising. Your team stops being able to predict what
happens next.

And this is what people mean when they say a Jet is hard to pin down. **Everything you said was
true when you said it.** That is not much comfort to somebody holding the third version of the plan.

### What you need from other people

**Room to move, and a straight answer about what actually matters.**

Give a Jet three equal priorities and they will optimise for whoever asked most recently. Tell a
Jet which one is genuinely first and they will protect it while flexing everything around it.

### What drives you mad

Rigidity for its own sake. Being made to follow a route that is plainly worse than the alternative
because the route was agreed in advance.

People who cannot move.

### The thing worth working on

**Write down what you committed to.**

Not for anyone else. For you.

Your problem is not that you break promises. It is that you make more of them than any person could
keep, at speed, in good faith, and you lose track of the total.

A list turns that from a character question into an arithmetic one.

## THE ROCKET
### Fast pace · Task priority

### How you move

Straight up, at speed.

Buckle in, we are not stopping. Show me the baby, I do not need the labour pains. From the ground
to where you are going with nothing in between and no interest in the scenery.

### What people notice

A stark office. Minimalist, or just empty. You communicate in bullets.

You read the first line of an email, jump to the last, and only go back to the middle if the two do
not add up. That is not carelessness. It is triage, and you are usually right about what matters.

Time is the resource you protect most fiercely, and you can tell within about ninety seconds
whether a meeting is going to be worth its length.

### Your gift

**In a genuine crisis, everybody wants a Rocket in charge.**

That is not a small thing. When it is going badly wrong, decisions get made, the team gets clarity,
and nobody stands around waiting to be told what to do. Everyone else is still working out who
should decide. You have decided.

**And you will say the uncomfortable thing out loud** while everyone else is deciding whether to.
Teams without a Rocket have problems that everybody knows about and nobody names. Teams with one do
not.

You also protect your team's time, which they may never thank you for and which matters more than
they know.

### Under pressure

**You run people over.**

Speed becomes the only value. Consultation gets cut because it is slow, and you decide alone
because deciding alone is faster. Things get done, and people leave.

Here is the part that is hard: **you often do not connect those two facts.** The delivery was
excellent. The attrition looks like a separate problem, or a people problem, or somebody else's
problem.

Your other pressure move is expecting the same intensity from everyone. You can run at that speed.
You have built a career on being able to. It does not follow that the person in front of you can,
and under pressure you stop checking.

### What you need from other people

**The headline first.**

A Rocket given a three-minute preamble stopped listening at minute one. Lead with the ask, follow
with the reasoning, and you will get a better answer faster than anybody else could have given you.

### What drives you mad

Meetings that could have been decisions. Being asked to explain something twice. Waiting for a
consensus that was never going to arrive.

People who confuse discussing a thing with doing it.

### The thing worth working on

**Ask one question before you decide.**

Not a consultation. Not a workshop. **One question, to one person who will be affected.**

It costs you about ninety seconds and it buys you two things: information you did not have, and a
person who now believes they were part of it rather than subject to it.

You will not be slower. You will be less alone.

# PART SIX · WORKING WITH THE OTHER THREE

**Every report includes all three of the styles the reader is not.**

⚠️ **This section is why everybody reads all four profiles.** The self-knowledge is the setup; this
is the payoff.

### Structure per pairing

For each of the other three styles, the report gives:

1. **What they need from you** — one paragraph
2. **What you are most likely to get wrong with them** — one paragraph, specific
3. **One adjustment** — a single concrete change, not a list

### The twelve pairings

| You are | Leading a | The one adjustment |
|---|---|---|
| **Rocket** | Tractor | Give them the context on Monday and the decision on Wednesday |
| **Rocket** | Bus | Ask how they are before you ask what you called about |
| **Rocket** | Jet | Tell them which priority is genuinely first |
| **Tractor** | Rocket | Lead with the answer, put your reasoning underneath it |
| **Tractor** | Bus | Say the thing you are thinking before you have finished thinking it |
| **Tractor** | Jet | Give them the destination and stop specifying the route |
| **Bus** | Rocket | Get to the ask in the first sentence |
| **Bus** | Tractor | Give them written detail, not a conversation |
| **Bus** | Jet | Pin down what they actually committed to, in writing |
| **Jet** | Rocket | Do not reframe it. Say it plainly |
| **Jet** | Tractor | Stop changing the plan and let one version run |
| **Jet** | Bus | Check before you promise something on their behalf |

## 7.1 Two tiers

⚠️ **A station manager has fifteen minutes. A founder has an evening.** The report must serve both.

**TIER ONE — the two-page summary.** Front of the document. Works alone.
**TIER TWO — the full report.** Everything else.

## 7.2 Full structure

| Part | Contents |
|---|---|
| **Summary** | 2 pages. Your position, your style, your pressure move, your range, your one adjustment |
| **I · About this** | What it measures, what it does not, how to read it. **Permission language and limits** |
| **II · The framework** | Two axes, four styles, why a fleet needs all four |
| **III · The four styles** | All four in full. **Everybody reads all four** |
| **IV · Your Imprint** | Your position with blend percentages. Your profile |
| **V · Under pressure** | Your pressure position, direction of movement, what your strength becomes |
| **VI · Your range** | Band, and the cost at both ends |

| **VIII · Your Fleet** | Team layer. Estimated or calculated |
| **IX · What now** | Practice, not insight. Three specific actions |

---

## 7.3 Report design rules

**No style is better.** Every gift is paired with its pressure cost. Every range band has a cost at
both ends.

⚠️ **The pressure section must sting slightly.** A report nobody flinches at is a horoscope. The
Rocket section has to say *things get done and people leave*, and it also has to say that in a
crisis everyone wants a Rocket in charge. Both, honestly, in the same section.

**Position over category.** Always show where somebody actually sits with percentages, never just
the box.

**Permission language, repeated.** *A starting point, not a box you are locked into.* Belbin's most
cited criticism is pigeonholing and this is the defence.

**Every section ends with something to do**, not something to know.

**Grade 7 to 8. No em dashes.**

# PART NINE · VALIDATION ROADMAP

**What to do, in order, to build a defensible validity case.**

⚠️ **Until step two is complete, the honest claim is "behaviourally grounded, validation in
progress."** That is a normal thing for a new instrument to say. Claiming more than that is the
thing that damages credibility with a sophisticated buyer.

| Stage | When | What | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Content validity** | **Now, during build** | Keep the item-to-construct mapping table. Every question, what it measures, why | Free if done as you go. Expensive to reconstruct. **Part Three is this record** |
| **Internal consistency** | First 100 responses | Cronbach's alpha per block. Target ≥ 0.70 | Do the Pace items actually move together? A standard number buyers ask for |
| **Item analysis** | First 100 | Item-total correlation per question. Drop or rewrite anything below 0.30 | Finds the questions that are not doing their job |
| **Distribution check** | First 300 | Are all four quadrants populated? | If 60% come out Rocket, an item is leading |
| **Test-retest** | 6 months | Same respondents, 6 weeks apart. Target r ≥ 0.75 on natural position | Stability. If style moves week to week it is not measuring a style |
| **Convergent validity** | 12 months | Correlate against an established instrument on a subsample | Do Rockets score high D on DISC? Expect moderate correlation, not high |
| **Criterion validity** | 18 months+ | Do observers rate people the way the instrument does? | The expensive one, and the strongest claim available |

**Document everything in a technical manual** kept separately from the report. That document is what
you hand an L&D director who asks the hard question, and having one is itself a credibility signal.
